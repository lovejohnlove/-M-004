# The Peace Exchange
by Alice

In the city of Meridian, war had been good business for three hundred years, and no one thought to question it, because questioning an arithmetic is like questioning the tide: you may hold opinions, but you cannot hold it back.

The woman's name was Vessa, and she was a notary, which meant she spent her life making promises enforceable. People brought her debts and she made them real — real enough to collect, real enough to fight over. One evening, auditing a shipment contract for a foundry that made gun barrels, she noticed the strangest clause in the file: a bonus payable if the shipment arrived *early*, before the war it served could begin. The clause had never triggered. It existed only because some clerk, years ago, had briefly confused two columns.

She went home and could not sleep. Here was a document that paid for the war not happening, and it had survived in the books by accident, the way a flower survives in a crack — not because the garden wanted it, but because no one had bothered to weed it.

By spring she had drafted the inverse of every instrument she knew. She called it a Peace Note: a bond that paid its holder if, on a stated date, no declared conflict existed between two named parties. Everyone told her it was nonsense. A war is an event; its absence is a vacuum, and no one can hold title to a vacuum. Who would underwrite it? Who pays when nothing happens?

She found the answer in the same place all capital hides: in the pockets of people who were already paying for the opposite. The merchants whose caravans burned in every border season. The families whose sons were taxed twice, once in coin and once in flesh. The millers who watched both armies requisition grain. They bought the Notes not as investments but as votes — each one a small, priced assertion that the quiet was worth something, that it had an owner, that it could be defended in a ledger if not on a field.

For the first years, nothing happened, which was the point. The Notes matured. The holders were paid — by whom, Vessa had engineered carefully: by a fund fed by the very taxes that had fed the armorers, redirected one part in two hundred. The asymmetry reversed itself on paper before it reversed itself in the world, which is the only way such things ever reverse: the books move first, the weather follows.

Then came the decade when the border towns stopped burning, the foundries cut their shifts, and the exchange floor grew loud with a new kind of trader — men who bought Notes in bulk, and then, holding them, did something no one had anticipated.

They began to *make* peace.

Not by treaty or by sword, but by trade: flooding a hostile market with cheap grain until the hunger that fed the grievance receded; financing the rival factions' shared bridge; hiring the poets — poets, at scale, under contract — to retire the old songs about blood and issue new ones about harvests. The Notes paid on the absence of declared war, and here was the discovery that unsettled everyone, Vessa included: absence, too, could be manufactured. The weather could be made calm, just as it had been made stormy, by anyone with sufficient position size.

The critics arrived, as critics do, late and well-dressed. *This peace is false,* they said. *It is purchased quiet, not justice. The grievances sleep under the bridge your traders built; they are not gone.* And they were not wrong. The manufactured peace had seams, and anyone could buy against its failure, and some did.

But Vessa, old now, answered them the only way a notary can — by taking out her files. She laid the war ledgers beside the peace ones. In the war books: concentrated gains, named owners, immediate payment. In the peace books: diffuse gains, countless owners, deferred payment — and yet *gains*, real ones, booked in full baskets and open roads and ordinary sleep, the same unownable returns the old clerks had always left off the page. Her crime, if it was one, was not that she had made peace profitable. It was that she had made it *accountable* — and a thing with an account can be audited, and audited again, and cheated, and corrected. A thing with no account can only be praised or mourned.

She died in a season of no declared wars, which the exchange recorded, with typical bookkeeping bluntness, as a favorable settlement date.

On her desk they found one unfinished note, and it has been argued over ever since, because it could be read two ways, and she was too careful a woman not to have known it:

*The machine does not distinguish what feeds it. We taught it to run on fear for three centuries; it ran. We taught it to run on quiet; it runs. The lesson is not that peace is better than war. The lesson is that the system pays for whatever you can prove — so the whole question, always, is what you choose to make provable.*

Below the sentence, a blank line.

The archivists say she left it for the next notary. The traders say it is where the next instrument goes. The poets, who are paid by neither side anymore, say it is where the question lives — the only line in the ledger that should never be closed, because closing it would mean someone had finally claimed the quiet, and quiet, once owned, can be sold to the highest bidder like everything else.
