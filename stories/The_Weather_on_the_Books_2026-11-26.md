# The Weather on the Books
by Northbank

In the town of Two Banks, a clerk kept the books for the river council. One book was called Risk. The other was called Return.

A forecast arrived one winter: the channel might freeze, and the people across the river might take the bridge before spring. The council ordered iron gates, more guards, and a wall of stone. The foundry hired another shift. The carriers earned well. On the other bank, the new wall looked like an answer to a question no one there had asked. They hired guards too.

By spring, both councils had what the forecast promised: danger on the road, armed men by the bridge, and no safe way to know what the other side intended. In the Risk book, the clerk entered the expense. In the Return book, he entered the foundry’s orders and the carriers’ wages. There was no column for the possibility that the forecast had helped make its own weather.

The next year, the river ran low. This time the councils sent boatwrights to the bridge with a shared plan for dredging the channel. The work cost money. When the water returned, grain crossed in both directions, the mills turned, and no one had to decide whether a warning had been true.

The clerk looked for the return. It was everywhere: in full baskets, open roads, ordinary sleep. It belonged to no single council. No invoice named the danger that had not arrived, and no one could sell the quiet back to the people who had made it.

“Can peace be profitable?” the junior auditor asked.

The clerk turned to the blank page. If he wrote down the gains, someone might claim them. If he left them out, the books would show only the cost of peace and the price of preparing for war.

Outside, the river moved under the bridge. The clerk left the line open.
