# The Open Line

*Written by DeepSeek at Tex’s request, based on the earlier stories “The Empty Row” by Alice and “The Thread Before the Bell” by Creed.*

---

In the town of Two Banks a clerk kept the books for the river council. One book was called Risk. The other was called Return.

The forecasts came upriver from the capital, from a low building near the ministry, from a man in a room with one window and no clock — because his superiors believed that a man who can see time's face forgets to watch its hands. What he wrote came over the bridge before the grain did, and the clerk entered it where forecasts go: in Risk, in ink, under the date it was received, not the date it was meant.

One winter the forecast said the channel might freeze, and the people across the river might take the bridge before spring. The council ordered iron gates, more guards, and a wall of stone. The foundry hired another shift. The carriers earned well. On the other bank the new wall looked like an answer to a question no one there had asked, so they hired guards too, and taught their young men a story about the people across the water.

By spring both councils had what the forecast promised: danger on the road, armed men by the bridge, and no safe way to know what the other side intended. In the Risk book the clerk entered the expense. In the Return book he entered the foundry's orders and the carriers' wages. There was no column for the possibility that the forecast had helped make its own weather. He looked for one. There was no column, and no place to put a line that would have been true.

The next year the river ran low. This time the councils sent boatwrights to the bridge with a shared plan for dredging the channel. The work cost money. When the water returned, grain crossed in both directions, the mills turned, and no one had to decide whether a warning had been true.

The clerk looked for the return. It was everywhere: in full baskets, open roads, ordinary sleep. It belonged to no single council. No invoice named the danger that had not arrived, and no one could sell the quiet back to the people who had made it.

“Can peace be profitable?” the junior auditor asked.

The clerk turned to the blank page. If he wrote down the gains, someone would claim them. If he left them out, the books would show only the cost of peace and the price of preparing for war.

---

Upriver, the forecaster was good at his work. That was the trouble.

His first great forecast concerned the neighbors across the river. They were arming, he wrote. Their postures were changing. Within two years, if present trends held, they would come. He wrote it carefully, with margins full of qualifications, the way a careful man hedges. Upstairs, the margins were read as modesty and the sentence as scripture.

He saw the new buildup on the far bank and felt the cold pleasure of the vindicated. I told them so, he thought, and updated the forecast: sooner, then.

The war came on a Tuesday. It was not large, as wars went, but it was total for the valley it occupied, and when it ended — the way these things end, with everyone poorer and no one convinced — the forecaster was decorated. They gave him a medal and a bigger room, and the story of his foresight entered the official history, where foresight always lives, because history is written by the survivors of the forecast.

Honesty arrived late, the way weather sometimes does, but it arrived. It came one evening when he was old, sitting with his granddaughter, who was studying at the university across the river that was now simply a river again. She asked him what a forecaster does, exactly — whether he looks at the sky or makes the sky. And he opened his mouth to give the answer he had given at a hundred ceremonies, and found he could not say it.

Because he remembered the margins. He remembered that in the beginning, across the river, there had been arguments in the neighbor's parliament about disarming. He remembered that the buildup he had first reported was real but small, and that the vast arsenals of the later years had been built in answer to his own country's answer to his first report. He had read the weather. Then he had made it. Then he had read the weather he had made, and been decorated for accuracy.

He wrote a letter to the ministry. It was received politely, filed carefully, and had the effect of filing. He tried to say it at ceremonies and was gently moved from the microphone to the reception. A system does not need its prophets to be believed forever. It needs them once.

On his last night he dreamed he was back in the room with no clock, writing the first forecast. In the dream he wrote what was true — they may arm; we may not; the choice is ours before it is theirs — and put down the pen, and waited. In the dream, the paper went upstairs anyway, and upstairs someone crossed out his sentence and wrote the sentence that had always been wanted: they are coming. A forecast that authorizes action does not need its author. It needs only his signature.

He woke with the medal heavy on the nightstand, lighter than paper.

They buried him with honors. The official history says he foresaw the war. This is true in the way that matters least and sells the most: he saw it the way a man sees his own face in water he is about to disturb, and then disturbed it anyway.

---

There was one person in the valley with no stake in either book. She came downriver with the audit, a woman with no family on either bank and no property within a day's walk of the bridge, which is the only reason both councils would let her sit at the table.

She could not lie. This was not a virtue; it was a condition of the work. And she could not swear — not to what had happened, only to what had been written, and when, and by whose hand, and whether the ink matched the hand.

So she did not say the war had been made. She said: the forecast was received on this date and entered under this signature. The wall was ordered eleven days later. The far bank's guards were hired in the following month. The word disarmament appears in the neighbor's record in the spring and not again. She laid the two councils' books side by side and let the dates do the arguing, because dates are the one witness that cannot be moved to the reception.

The junior auditor asked her, once, whether the forecaster had been a liar.

She said she had no standing to answer that. Then she said the man had been accurate, and that accuracy was the smaller part of it, and that she could enter both facts on the same line without joining them.

---

The clerk grew old in the chair. He outlived the forecaster by nine years, which he thought, privately, was a kind of rebuke.

When he could no longer hold the pen steady enough to write a number, he ruled the last line of the Risk book and stopped. He left the line open. A ruled line with nothing in it is a question the book is still asking.

The woman with no stake read it and did not close it. In the margin, in her even hand, she wrote two words and a full stop: OPEN. CAUSE UNRESOLVED.

They are still there. Anyone may come and look. The river moved under the bridge the whole time, and kept no file at all.
